# The software wasn't there

*Steve Iverson, October 2026*

I've had my IBM PCjr since 1984. It has a 4.77 MHz 8088, 16 colors, and a
three-voice sound chip. Its whole life, I thought I knew what it could do.

I was wrong. In three weeks this October it learned to run a Doom-style
shooter, with zombiemen and imps, doors, a red key and a status bar with a
face. Doom came out in December 1993. The PCjr shipped in early 1984. That
is almost ten years in which the machine could have done it and nobody
knew how. Even Wolfenstein 3D, the simpler game that started it all, was
eight years away.

It isn't the only one.

- **Sopwith** is a 1984 game. It was written for machines like this one.
  Rebuilt from its own released source, with no change to how it plays,
  it now runs 60% faster on the same PCjr: 10.5 frames a second to 16.8.
  Nothing new was added to the hardware. The speed was always there,
  sitting in the code.
- **Shreddy**, a dirt jump rider doing tricks in a smooth, seamless
  one-minute loop, with the bike's sound on the chip, would have stopped
  people in their tracks at a computer store in 1984.
- **A flight simulator, a Mandelbrot zoom, a demoscene demo that talks,**
  speech from a sound chip that was never meant to speak, and a few
  classic arcade games written while I sat at the PCjr's own keyboard and
  asked for them.

The silicon was ready in 1984. The software wasn't. What was missing was
the ideas, the techniques, and the sheer hours it takes to write and tune
a program like these in assembly on a slow machine. Those took the rest of
the industry a decade to work out.

What changed is AI. I didn't hand-write these programs. I described what I
wanted, and Claude wrote them, tested them in an emulator, ran them on the
real PCjr over a WiFi modem, looked at pictures of the screen, and fixed
what was wrong. The skills that were scarce in 1984 aren't scarce anymore.

## So what about today?

Here is the part I keep thinking about. If a 1984 computer was holding a
decade of software it never got to run, what is today's hardware holding?

We have machines thousands of times faster than the PCjr, in our pockets.
Everything is networked. There are sensors, GPUs, and cloud computers you
can rent by the second. I'm sure the gap between what this hardware can do
and what our software actually does with it is at least as big as it was
in 1984. Probably far bigger.

Until now, closing that gap took years and big teams. Now the bottleneck
is moving from "can we build it" to "can we imagine it". AI makes writing
software, even large and complicated software, faster and open to far
more people. Projects that used to be too big to start are now a few
weekends.

So the question isn't only "what could we have done in 1984?" It's "what
could we be doing right now, with what's already on our desks, that nobody
has written yet?" I think the answer is nearly unlimited. Software is
about to get much better: faster, with fewer bugs, and built for exactly
what each person needs.

We have to widen our idea of what's possible. It's going to be a really
neat time.
